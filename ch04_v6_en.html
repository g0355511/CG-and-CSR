Chapter 4. How Do We Know the Truth? Disclosure and Auditing

Opening Case: Toshiba's Financial Fraud — Why Did the Audit Committee Miss It?

In July 2015, Toshiba's Independent Investigation Committee released a report finding that Toshiba had overstated profits by ¥151.8 billion (approximately NT$40 billion) between 2008 and 2014. The practice was systematic, spanned several business divisions, and involved corporate-level management (Toshiba Independent Investigation Committee, 2015).

Toshiba had four independent directors, twice the required number, as well as audit, compensation, and nomination committees. Yet the investigation found that the audit committee failed to detect the fraud and that its oversight was ineffective. The board's culture encouraged endorsement in place of questioning.

More remarkably, Toshiba's external auditors issued unmodified opinions throughout those seven years, indicating that they considered the financial statements fairly presented.

The case frames three questions for this chapter.

  1. How can shareholders and investors tell whether the information they receive is reliable? Did Toshiba's audit committee lack expertise, independence, or both?
  2. Why can an external audit fail? The auditors issued unmodified opinions for seven years. Did the problem lie in the audit procedures or in auditor independence?
  3. What can users rely on when professional gatekeepers themselves prove unreliable?

Chapter overview. Chapter 3 examined the board's role in overseeing management. That role depends on information. How can directors tell whether management has given them an accurate account? This chapter examines the reliability of the information on which oversight depends, through disclosure, financial reporting, and external auditing. Section 4 considers why investors, companies, and people affected by business activities need sustainability information and how they can assess its credibility. Section 5 examines accountability for misstatements.

Learning objectives

  1. Explain how information asymmetry harms investors and markets (Section 1).
  2. Describe financial reporting principles, verification mechanisms, and internal control (Section 2).
  3. Analyze how external audits can fail and how auditor independence can be safeguarded (Section 3).
  4. Explain what sustainability information different users need and compare disclosure and verification across the two types of reporting (Section 4).
  5. Explain why accountability matters when disclosures are misleading (Section 5).

Section 1. How Does Information Asymmetry Harm Investors and Markets?

When shareholders cannot directly monitor management, they depend on company disclosures. Markets need reliable information to function. This section examines where information asymmetry comes from and what it does to markets.

1. The Nature of Information Asymmetry

Information asymmetry occurs when parties to a transaction have access to information of different quantity or quality. In capital markets, management knows the company's operations in detail, while outside shareholders and investors depend on what the company discloses.

Healy and Palepu (2001) distinguish three sources of information asymmetry.

(1) The advantage of inside information. Managers see day-to-day operations and know about unpublished R&D progress, potential litigation, and the risk of losing customers. These facts can affect share prices while remaining invisible to outside investors.

(2) Disclosure costs and strategic considerations. Preparing reports and obtaining legal advice costs money, and extensive disclosure can expose commercially sensitive information. Management may disclose favorable information selectively and withhold unfavorable information.

(3) Difficulty interpreting information. Even when companies disclose information, understanding the risks in complex derivatives, related-party transactions, and contingent liabilities may require specialist knowledge.

2. Consequences of Information Asymmetry

(1) Adverse selection. When investors cannot distinguish higher-quality companies from lower-quality ones, they will pay a price reflecting average quality. That undervalues stronger firms and overvalues weaker ones. Eventually, stronger firms may withdraw, leaving weaker firms in the market (Akerlof, 1970).

Example. When investors cannot tell whether financial statements are reliable, they may demand a higher risk premium. This raises financing costs even for companies that report honestly.

(2) Moral hazard. When managers' actions are hard to observe, they may pursue personal benefits at shareholders' expense. An example is empire-building through acquisitions that increase management compensation without creating shareholder value.

(3) Lower market liquidity. A lack of confidence in company disclosures can widen bid–ask spreads and increase trading costs. Investors trade less, liquidity falls, and share prices become more volatile. This can harm transparent companies as well, because investors struggle to tell them apart from opaque ones (Akerlof, 1970).

3. Mechanisms for Addressing Information Asymmetry

Companies can improve the reliability of information as they produce it, and market mechanisms can help users interpret and verify it after disclosure. Legal liability supports both.

(1) Internal mechanisms

Mechanism Function Limitation
Internal control Promotes reliable reporting and compliance. See the five COSO components in Section 2.3. The company designs and operates its own controls, which limits their independence.
Internal audit Independently evaluates risk management and governance processes. See Section 2.4. Management determines compensation.

(2) Market mechanisms

Mechanism Function Limitation
Mandatory disclosure Requires companies to disclose financial information periodically. Sets a minimum standard but cannot ensure accuracy.
External audit Independent auditors examine the reliability of financial statements. See Section 3. Auditor independence can be compromised.
Credit ratings Rating agencies assess a company's credit risk. Companies pay the agencies, creating a conflict of interest.
Analysts Analysts research companies and publish reports. They may face pressure to issue optimistic reports.
Media and whistleblowers Bring corporate misconduct to light. Detection depends on the circumstances of individual cases.

(3) Legal liability as a backstop. Liability for financial misstatements gives companies and individuals a reason to report honestly. Section 5 and Appendix 2 explain how accountability works. Disclosure and auditing alone may leave false statements undetected for some time.

Key takeaway. Each mechanism has limits. Mandatory disclosure makes information available, as Toshiba's formally compliant filings illustrate, but a filing can still contain inflated profits. External audit supports information quality, though independence can be compromised. Rating agencies and analysts face conflicts of interest. Media and whistleblowing depend on individual cases. Mandatory disclosure, external audit, and legal liability therefore work together to make information available, support its quality, and give misreporting consequences.

Think about it. Which mechanism do you think addresses information asymmetry most effectively? Why?

Section 2. What Are the Principles of Financial Reporting, Who Verifies It, and Who Is Responsible?

Assessing the reliability of financial information starts with understanding what companies report and the rules they follow. Accounting standards give management a common language for preparing financial statements and leave room for judgment that can be abused. This section introduces the statements and reporting principles, explains how companies prepare and oversee them, and uses a year-end inventory discrepancy to illustrate the division of responsibilities.

1. Components of Financial Statements

A complete set includes:

  1. Balance sheet. Shows financial position at a particular date (assets = liabilities + equity).
  2. Statement of comprehensive income. Shows performance over a period (revenue − expenses = net income).
  3. Statement of cash flows. Shows cash inflows and outflows over a period.
  4. Statement of changes in equity. Shows movements in equity over a period.
  5. Notes. Provide details such as accounting policies, significant estimates, and related-party transactions.

Example. Each component can help readers spot a different type of problem. Toshiba illustrates the first two.

Component What to look for Why it matters
Balance sheet Financial position at a point in time Toshiba overstated inventory, inflating assets.
Statement of comprehensive income Performance over a period Toshiba recognized revenue early and deferred expenses, overstating operating profit.
Statement of cash flows Actual cash movements Cash flows are harder to manipulate through estimates and can expose discrepancies.
Statement of changes in equity Movements in shareholders' equity Shows distributions of earnings and changes in share capital.
Notes Policies and significant estimates Assumptions and changes in estimates can offer clues to misreporting.

2. Basic Principles of Financial Reporting

  1. Accrual basis. Transactions are recognized when they occur, regardless of when cash changes hands. This better reflects their economic substance but leaves room for manipulation, such as recognizing revenue too early or deferring expenses.
  2. Going concern. Financial statements assume the company will continue operating. A risk of business failure must be disclosed.
  3. Materiality. Material information should be disclosed separately, while immaterial information may be aggregated. Management exercises judgment about what is material.
  4. Conservatism. Under uncertainty, accounting for assets and earnings calls for caution. In practice, management can depart from this principle by overstating assets or understating liabilities.

Four accounting issues in the Toshiba case

Worked case | What does Toshiba’s overstated project revenue tell us?

In the Toshiba case, estimated total costs were understated on some long-term projects. Suppose a contract has revenue of 100 and costs incurred to date of 40. Evidence supports estimated total costs of 80, yet the company uses 64. These figures are illustrative. If a cost-based measure of progress is appropriate, the estimates produce cumulative revenue of 50 and 62.5, respectively.

  1. Accrual basis: How much revenue should be recognized by period-end? Revenue on this project is recognized as work progresses. Supported progress is 40 ÷ 80 = 50%, so cumulative revenue is 50. When the customer pays is a separate question.
  2. Conservatism (prudence): What supports the cost estimate? A reduction in estimated total costs to 64 calls for evidence of expected savings, such as lower purchase prices or fewer labor hours. Understating total costs raises reported progress from 50% to 62.5%. Prudence means using evidence to address uncertainty and avoiding unsupported understatement of assets or income as well.
  3. Materiality: Whose judgment could the overstatement of 12.5 affect? Consider the amount in the context of the financial statements as a whole. If the correction turns reported profit into a loss, investors may reassess the company’s performance. Consider the size, nature, and circumstances of this and other misstatements, both individually and together. Apply this question to every row below.
  4. Going concern: After correction, should we investigate the company’s ability to keep operating? Review cash-flow forecasts, debt maturities, and available financing. If higher project costs mean the company may be unable to repay debts as they fall due, assess whether material uncertainty exists, what disclosure is needed, and whether the going concern basis remains appropriate. The revenue difference alone cannot resolve these questions.

Knowing how much judgment management exercises under these principles helps readers assess reported figures. Two companies in the same industry with the same revenue can present quite different financial statements.

3. How Are Financial Statements Prepared, and How Do Companies Prevent Errors?

The first two subsections explained what the statements contain and the principles for recording transactions. Management prepares the statements and establishes and maintains the associated controls. Operating and finance teams perform those controls in their daily work.

For a raw materials purchase, an authorized person approves it in advance. After delivery, staff match the purchase order, receiving record, and invoice. After the entry is recorded, a reviewer checks the amount and accounting period. At period-end, finance staff reconcile the accounts and make necessary adjustments as part of closing the books. If the company received the materials and incurred the payment obligation in December, it records the inventory and payable in December even if the invoice arrives in January.

(1) Internal control

Internal control comprises the processes a company designs and operates to provide reasonable assurance about operational effectiveness, reporting reliability, and compliance. The Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO) identifies five interrelated components in its Internal Control – Integrated Framework (2013).

Component Description
Control environment Ethics standards, board oversight, and clear responsibilities establish the basis for effective controls.
Risk assessment Identify where errors could occur, such as failing to record goods received before year-end.
Control activities Take specific measures such as approving purchases, matching documents, and reviewing entries.
Information and communication Give the people who process transactions timely access to information, such as when warehouse staff send receiving records to finance.
Monitoring activities Check on an ongoing or periodic basis whether controls work and follow up on deficiencies.

(2) Management's responsibilities

Management is primarily responsible for financial reporting and internal control. It assigns responsibilities, identifies material errors and control deficiencies, and sees that they are corrected. Sections 302 and 404 of the U.S. Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) and Taiwan's Regulations Governing Establishment of Internal Control Systems by Public Companies illustrate reporting certification and internal control responsibilities. See Section 5 and Appendix 2 for liability arising from misstatements.

4. Who Assesses Controls, Oversees Reporting, and Audits the Statements?

Once controls are in place, who checks that they work, follows up on significant problems, and audits the published statements?

(1) Internal audit assesses control effectiveness. Internal audit independently evaluates risk management, controls, and governance processes. In procurement, auditors could sample receiving records to check whether finance staff matched documents as required and followed up on discrepancies. They report deficiencies and monitor remedial action.

(2) The audit committee oversees financial reporting. The committee can review internal audit findings and ask management how material errors arose, whether they were corrected, and who will address the underlying deficiencies. It can also discuss significant audit matters with the external auditor and follow up on differences between management's explanations and audit findings.

(3) The external auditor audits the financial statements. The auditor obtains evidence and expresses an independent opinion on whether the statements are fairly presented, in all material respects, under the applicable reporting framework. Readers can start with the type of opinion and then read the basis for it.

Audit opinion Meaning
Unmodified opinion The financial statements are fairly presented in all material respects.
Qualified opinion Identified misstatements are material but not pervasive, or insufficient evidence leaves open the possibility of effects that are material but not pervasive.
Adverse opinion Identified misstatements are both material and pervasive.
Disclaimer of opinion The auditor cannot obtain sufficient appropriate evidence, and the possible effects could be both material and pervasive.

5. The Year-End Inventory Count Is Short by 100 Units. Who Does What?

Suppose the records show 1,000 units of raw materials, but warehouse staff count 900. The missing units may have been issued without an accounting entry, or the physical count may have missed them. The company needs to determine the cause before deciding whether to adjust the financial statements.

Question Who handles it?
Do the accounts need adjustment? Warehouse and finance staff recount the inventory and compare materials requisitions with the accounting records. Management uses the findings to ensure that any necessary adjustments are made.
Why did the discrepancy go undetected until year-end? Internal audit examines materials issue records, entries, and periodic reconciliations. For a significant control deficiency, the audit committee can ask management to explain the cause, propose remedies, and report progress.
Could the discrepancy affect the audit opinion? The external auditor obtains evidence on inventory quantities and amounts, evaluates the adjustment, and considers whether any uncorrected difference or gap in evidence is material.

Returning to Toshiba, the company had an audit committee and received unmodified audit opinions, yet material misstatements went undetected. Section 3 examines what can compromise auditor independence and the limitations that remain even when the auditor is independent.

Section 3. Why Can External Audits Fail?

The year-end inventory example in Section 2 showed that the external auditor must obtain evidence independently to assess whether the statements need adjustment. Two questions help explain how an audit can fail. Can auditors exercise professional judgment when a client pressures them? If they can, how strong a conclusion does their evidence support?

1. Why Can Clients Influence Auditors?

Suppose a company hints that it will replace its audit firm if the firm issues a qualified opinion. The opinion matters to the company's public reporting, while reappointment and fees matter to the firm. Auditors must form an opinion based on evidence under these pressures. Four situations illustrate threats to independence (DeFond & Zhang, 2014).

(1) Self-interest threat. An auditor has a financial interest in the client relationship. A firm that provides consulting or tax services alongside its audit may earn more from those services than from the audit and hesitate to challenge the client.

Example. Enron paid Arthur Andersen substantial audit and consulting fees. When Enron's financial problems emerged, Andersen went along with Enron's accounting treatment. Andersen was ultimately indicted in connection with the destruction of audit workpapers, and the global Big Five accounting firms became the Big Four.

(2) Self-review threat. An auditor examines the firm's own work or work performed as a non-audit service. One example is auditing internal controls that the firm designed for the client.

(3) Familiarity threat. A close relationship can impair independence. A partner who has served a client for more than a decade may develop personal relationships with management that affect professional judgment.

(4) Intimidation threat. A client pressures the auditor, for example by threatening to replace the firm if it issues a qualified opinion.

2. How Do Institutions Safeguard Auditor Independence?

How much pressure can individual resolve withstand when a client threatens to replace the firm? Safeguards can address who takes part in appointing the auditor, what other services the firm may provide, and what information outsiders can see.

Safeguard Threat addressed How it works
Audit partner rotation Familiarity Periodic rotation gives an incoming partner a chance to reconsider previous judgments.
Restrictions on certain non-audit services Self-interest and self-review Reduce dependence on additional fees and help prevent firms from auditing work they designed or prepared.
Disclosure of audit and non-audit fees Self-interest Gives audit committees and investors information to ask whether other business relationships impair independence.
Audit committee participation in appointment and oversight Intimidation and self-interest Reduces management's ability to influence the auditor's judgment by threatening to replace the firm.

Peer review and other quality oversight examine how firms manage quality and perform engagements. They can improve audit quality and help assess how independence requirements work in practice.

3. Why Can an Independent Auditor Still Miss a Material Misstatement?

Return to the inventory count. The records show 1,000 units, and warehouse staff count 900. If the company provides only its own count sheet, the auditor still needs to establish how much inventory was there and whether the discrepancy arose from unrecorded issues of materials. Depending on risk and materiality, the auditor can observe the count, make test counts, or compare the inventory records with materials requisitions and the ledger.

Auditors select items for testing based on risk. Sampling can miss problems, and managers who collude to falsify documents can make separate records appear consistent. An estimate such as an inventory write-down requires the auditor to evaluate the assumptions and supporting evidence. Audits also have deadlines. When evidence is insufficient, the auditor considers alternative procedures and how any remaining evidence gap affects the opinion.

A financial statement audit provides reasonable assurance. The auditor obtains sufficient appropriate evidence to reduce the risk of issuing an inappropriate opinion on materially misstated financial statements to an acceptably low level. This is a high level of assurance, with some audit risk remaining.

Now suppose the information to be verified is a supplier's emissions instead of warehouse inventory. Why would users need that figure, and where could the company obtain evidence to support it? Section 4 begins with these questions.

Section 4. Why Do We Need Sustainability Information, and How Can We Assess Its Credibility?

The first three sections examined how investors and boards obtain reliable financial information. Sustainability disclosure starts with a further question. What facts beyond the information already in the financial statements could change people's decisions?

Imagine two companies with similar profits this year. One depends heavily on a plant in a water-scarce area. Investors and lenders need to know how much water the plant uses, whether it can keep operating during restrictions, and whether the company has an alternative supply. These facts affect estimates of future cash flows and the company's ability to repay debt. The board can also use this information to decide whether to invest in water-saving equipment or adjust capacity. Such risks can influence decisions before losses appear in the financial statements.

Water withdrawals and discharges can also affect residents. Residents need to know how much water the plant withdraws, how it monitors discharges, and what it does when readings are abnormal. This helps them assess effects on household water supplies and the environment. The same issue arises in supply chains. A brand needs to know what safety deficiencies a contract manufacturer has so it can decide how to address them. Workers need to know whether the hazards have been removed.

Companies and suppliers often hold the underlying data, which outsiders may struggle to obtain. Two reported figures may also be hard to compare if they cover different sites or periods or use different estimation methods. Sustainability disclosure therefore needs to establish what to disclose, how to prepare the figures, and what evidence users can rely on. The subsections that follow examine reporting standards, materiality, and assurance.

What is a sustainability report? It is a nonfinancial report that describes a company's environmental, social, and governance (ESG) practices and performance, including how it affects society and the environment as well as how much it earns.

  • Environmental: Carbon emissions, energy use, water, waste, and biodiversity
  • Social: Working conditions, supply chain responsibility, community engagement, and customer protection
  • Governance: Board operations, business integrity and anti-corruption, risk management, and human rights due diligence

Financial reports concentrate on quantitative financial information about past periods. Sustainability reports contain quantitative and qualitative information about past performance and future plans. Can the language and verification methods of financial reporting be carried over? New areas of disclosure also create new opportunities for greenwashing.

1. Financial Reports and Sustainability Reports

Dimension Financial reports Sustainability reports
Purpose Show financial performance and position Show ESG performance
Mandatory status Required by law or regulation Requirements vary. Sustainability reporting became mandatory for all Taiwan-listed companies starting in 2025.
Reporting standards International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) Multiple frameworks coexist, including GRI, SASB, TCFD, and ISSB standards.
Assurance or audit Mandatory external audit providing reasonable assurance Assurance engagements typically provide limited assurance. See Section 4.2.
Primary users Investors and creditors Stakeholders including employees, customers, communities, and nongovernmental organizations
Materiality Financial materiality Financial materiality under ISSB standards or double materiality under ESRS. See Section 4.5.

Nature of information Highly quantitative Quantitative and qualitative

2. Levels of Assurance and How They Differ from a Financial Audit

Financial statements are audited, while sustainability reports often undergo other assurance engagements. To understand the difference, consider the level of assurance the practitioner provides and how the practitioner expresses the conclusion.

(1) Reasonable assurance — used in financial statement audits

  • Meaning. The practitioner performs enough procedures to support a positive conclusion about material misstatement.
  • Form of conclusion. The practitioner gives a positive opinion, such as “In our opinion, the financial statements present fairly, in all material respects, in accordance with …”
  • Procedures. The practitioner obtains an understanding of controls, tests controls as applicable, and performs substantive procedures such as confirmations, inventory procedures, document checks, and evaluation of estimates.
  • Cost. Obtaining sufficient evidence for a positive opinion takes time and resources.

(2) Limited assurance — common in sustainability reporting

  • Meaning. The practitioner performs less extensive procedures and reaches a conclusion based on what came to their attention.
  • Form of conclusion. The practitioner states a conclusion in negative form, such as “Based on the procedures performed, nothing has come to our attention that causes us to believe the information was not prepared in accordance with …”
  • Procedures. These emphasize inquiry and analytical procedures, with less extensive substantive verification.
  • How to read the conclusion. It describes what the practitioner found within the procedures performed. It provides a lower level of assurance.

(3) Comparing the two levels

Dimension Reasonable assurance (audit) Limited assurance (review or assurance engagement)
Conclusion Positive form: “In our opinion … presents fairly.” Negative form: “Nothing has come to our attention …”
Evidence Sufficient appropriate evidence to support a positive opinion Evidence to support a limited-assurance conclusion
Procedures Control testing and substantive procedures, such as confirmations and inventory checks Primarily inquiries and analytical procedures
Scope Systematic coverage of material items More limited procedures focused on material matters
Cost More procedures, time, and cost Fewer procedures, generally quicker and less costly
Use Required for financial statement audits Often voluntary or required for selected sustainability disclosures

(4) Why limited assurance is more common for sustainability reports

  • Cost. Reasonable assurance requires substantial verification work, and companies may elect limited assurance to manage cost.
  • Nature of the evidence. Independent evidence can be difficult to obtain for supplier working conditions or upstream emissions.
  • Feasibility. A broad claim such as “We value employee rights” is difficult to verify in a way that supports a positive conclusion.

Example. For sustainability reporting by Taiwan-listed companies, limited assurance is common where assurance is obtained. Mandatory financial statement audits provide reasonable assurance.

Key takeaway. Check which sustainability figures were covered by assurance and what level the practitioner provided. An emissions figure under limited assurance can look just like an audited financial figure on the page, although the assurance they carry is quite different.

3. Challenges in Sustainability Reporting

(1) Multiple reporting standards. Companies use frameworks such as the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI), the Sustainability Accounting Standards Board (SASB) Standards, the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) recommendations, or standards issued by the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB). Companies can choose frameworks that suit them, making comparison difficult.

Example. Two companies reporting emissions under GRI and SASB may use different definitions or boundaries. A common global baseline could improve comparability. Section 4.4 discusses the effort to develop one.

(2) Uneven data quality. Sustainability data is often less consistent than financial data. Emissions may be estimated instead of measured, and supply chain data may depend on suppliers' own reports without independent verification.

(3) Greenwashing risk. Companies may exaggerate environmental performance or selectively disclose favorable information.

Example. German prosecutors fined DWS, Deutsche Bank's asset management subsidiary, €25 million in 2025 over overstated ESG marketing claims. DWS presented itself as an ESG leader, while only a small share of assets was subject to strict ESG criteria. The closing case examines this episode.

Some issues fall outside a report's definition of materiality and therefore may be omitted entirely. Section 4.5 examines how materiality sets the scope of disclosure.

4. The ISSB's Harmonization Effort and Four Pillars

When companies choose different frameworks, their data can be hard to compare. In 2023 the ISSB issued IFRS S1, which sets general requirements for sustainability-related financial disclosures, and IFRS S2, which addresses climate-related disclosures. The standards aim to provide a global baseline. Their structure builds on the TCFD recommendations.

Pillar Content
Governance Governance processes and controls for overseeing sustainability-related risks and opportunities
Strategy Effects of sustainability-related risks and opportunities on the business model, strategy, and financial planning
Risk management Processes for identifying, assessing, and managing sustainability-related risks and opportunities
Metrics and targets Measures and targets for tracking sustainability-related risks and opportunities, including greenhouse gas emissions

By 2026, countries including Nigeria (from 2024), Chile (from 2026), and Brazil had mandated use of ISSB standards. Mexico began applying them for the 2025 financial year, with first reports in 2026. Taiwan planned three phases starting with the 2026 financial year. 5. Double Materiality

The definition of materiality determines what a company discloses and whose interests the report serves. A company that asks only whether an issue affects its ability to earn money may omit its carbon emissions or suppliers' working conditions. Asking how the company affects people and the environment broadens the scope.

Type of materiality Definition Primary users
Financial materiality How sustainability matters affect company financial performance Investors and creditors
Impact materiality How company activities affect people and the environment Employees, communities, nongovernmental organizations, and regulators
  • ISSB (IFRS S1 and S2). Focuses on financial materiality given its investor-oriented audience.
  • European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS), under the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD). Apply double materiality, assessing financial and impact materiality.

The chosen definition determines what enters the report. Under a financial-materiality lens, some effects on society or the environment may go undisclosed. The scope of reporting therefore matters alongside the accuracy of what is published.

Think about it. Should sustainability reporting prioritize financial materiality or impact materiality? Why?

Section 5. How Can Those Responsible for Misleading Disclosures Be Held Accountable?

Section 1 introduced legal liability as a backstop. Toshiba illustrates how accounting treatments can mislead financial statement users. The closing case, DWS, concerns a gap between the sustainability investment processes an asset manager described publicly and what it did in practice. In both cases, ask who made the statement, who relied on it, and what records could establish what happened.

Accountability starts with identifying the document or public statement containing the misstatement. The applicable rules determine who was obliged to prepare, certify, audit, or disclose it and whether the conduct, harm, and evidence meet the relevant requirements. Civil damages can compensate those who suffered loss where the legal conditions are met. Regulatory sanctions and criminal liability apply under their respective rules and raise the cost of violations.

Chapter Summary

Information asymmetry is inherent in capital markets. Mandatory disclosure makes information available, auditing supports its quality, and legal liability gives misreporting consequences. Each plays a part. Toshiba's filings met formal requirements and its auditors issued unmodified opinions year after year, yet it still overstated profits by tens of billions of yen.

Answers to three core questions

  1. How can users gain confidence in reported information? Mandatory disclosure, external auditing, and legal liability reinforce each other.
  2. Why can external audits fail? Self-interest, self-review, familiarity, and intimidation can threaten independence. Sampling, fraud, estimation uncertainty, and time constraints place limits on the work even when auditors remain independent. Audits provide reasonable assurance.
  3. Can financial reporting methods transfer to sustainability reporting? The transfer remains incomplete. Sustainability reports use multiple standards, data quality varies, and assurance is often more limited. Institutions also differ on what counts as material.

Closing Case: DWS Greenwashing — Disclosure Credibility and the Challenge of Verifying ESG Data

Core question. Who verifies disclosures that go beyond financial reporting?

Case overview

DWS Group is a Deutsche Bank asset management subsidiary headquartered in Frankfurt, Germany. One of Europe's largest asset managers, it had about €800 billion under management in 2020.

Timeline

In its 2020 annual report, DWS said that about half of its assets, around €400 billion, were invested using ESG criteria. In 2021, former chief sustainability officer Desiree Fixler alleged to Germany's financial regulator, BaFin, that DWS had overstated the size of its ESG investments and that only a small portion of assets had been subject to strict ESG criteria. According to the account in this chapter, Fixler was dismissed in connection with her whistleblowing and subsequently raised complaints with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and German regulators.

In May 2022, German police searched DWS headquarters and Deutsche Bank offices to investigate possible prospectus fraud.

In 2023, DWS reached a settlement with the SEC. Without admitting or denying the SEC's findings, DWS agreed to pay **US19million * *overmisleadingstatementsaboutitsESGinvestmentprocessbetween2018and2021.AseparateUS6 million penalty related to anti-money-laundering deficiencies brought the total to US$25 million.

In April 2025, the Frankfurt Public Prosecutor's Office imposed a €25 million fine over exaggerated ESG marketing claims. DWS acknowledged in a statement that its marketing had at times been overly enthusiastic.

Implications for disclosure

Issue Implication
Gap between disclosure and reality DWS's disclosure was consistent with an internal methodology that was itself too permissive.
Role of whistleblowers Fixler's dismissal highlights the importance of whistleblower protection.
Regulatory response The SEC and German prosecutors both took action. Were the penalties sufficient?
Verification of sustainability data The case illustrates gaps in the mechanisms for verifying sustainability information.

As companies disclose more sustainability information, verification practices also need to develop. The scrutiny of ESG disclosures increasingly extends to evidence of what companies do in practice.

Discussion questions

  1. Information quality. How does DWS's greenwashing differ from Toshiba's financial fraud? Which is harder to detect, and which does greater harm to investors?
  2. Information asymmetry and verification. How do the two cases differ in information asymmetry and audit or verification mechanisms? Consider management's withholding of information from the board in Toshiba and DWS's withholding of information from investors. Why did Toshiba's external auditor miss the fraud while DWS's greenwashing came to light? Which form of information asymmetry do you consider more serious?
  3. Role of independent directors. If you were a DWS independent director, how would you respond to Fixler's allegations? Would you support applying the internal methodology or requiring stricter ESG criteria?
  4. Reliability of sustainability data. How would you improve the reliability of sustainability disclosures? What verification mechanisms would you require?
  5. Limits of ISSB standards. Can a common set of ISSB standards resolve the problem of greenwashing? Why or why not?

Appendix 1: Toshiba's Four Accounting Issues in Detail

This appendix follows the order of the table in Section 2. The case facts are drawn from Chapters 3–6 of the Toshiba Independent Investigation Committee's 2015 report. The amounts below are illustrative and show how the accounting treatments affect current-period income, expenses, or assets. Investigation report (English)

1. How does understating costs overstate revenue on long-term projects?

Toshiba used percentage-of-completion accounting for certain long-term projects. The investigation found that some total cost estimates omitted recent cost information or included projected savings without concrete support. In some cases, Toshiba also delayed recognizing losses that it could already foresee. These are two distinct judgments: how much work has been completed and whether the contract as a whole is expected to make a loss.

Suppose the contract price is 120 and cumulative costs incurred at period-end are 40. Holding other factors constant, measuring progress by costs gives the following results.

Period-end estimate Costs incurred ÷ estimated total costs Cumulative revenue
Supported total cost of 100 40 ÷ 100 = 40% 120 × 40% = 48
Understated total cost of 80 40 ÷ 80 = 50% 120 × 50% = 60

The work performed is the same. Changing the denominator from 100 to 80 increases cumulative revenue by 12. If the latest estimate on a different contract shows that total costs will exceed contract revenue, the expected loss must be reflected in the period required under the applicable accounting rules. Delaying recognition overstates current-period results.

2. Why might promotional expenses be recognized before payment?

The investigation found that the visual products business sometimes deferred operating expenses or amounts that should have been accrued in order to meet earnings targets. Consider a sales promotion.

Suppose a retailer completes the contracted promotion in December, and the company receives the invoice and pays 10 in January. If the payment obligation arose by year-end and the amount can be reasonably estimated, the company records the expense and corresponding liability of 10 in December. In January, payment reduces both cash and the liability by 10. Deferring the expense until January understates expenses and liabilities by 10 in the year ending in December and overstates pretax profit by 10. Accrual accounting looks to the period in which the obligation and expense arise.

3. Why does closing inventory affect profit?

Semiconductor production costs are allocated between units sold and units still in production or unsold at period-end. The investigation found that when standard costs were revised for some production stages, the standards for later stages were not updated accordingly. Too much cost remained in closing inventory. There were also problems with the disposal or write-down of some inventory retained for customer support after production ended.

Suppose 100 in costs is available for allocation. If closing inventory should be 30, cost of sales is 70. If closing inventory is reported at 40, cost of sales falls to 60. The balance sheet carries 10 more as inventory, the income statement shows 10 less in cost of sales, and gross profit rises by 10. For inventory that may need a write-down, the question is whether its recoverable value supports the carrying amount. A write-down is recognized when required.

4. Why is the markup on computer parts supplied to a manufacturer not yet current-period profit?

Through group companies, Toshiba purchased key parts and supplied them to contract manufacturers at prices above acquisition cost. The manufacturers incorporated the parts into computers and returned the finished goods to Toshiba. The higher supply price served to mask the actual procurement cost. The investigation found that Toshiba used the markup to reduce manufacturing costs when it supplied the parts, without properly eliminating the unrealized profit at period-end. This overstated current profit.

Suppose Toshiba buys a part for 50, supplies it to a manufacturer at 80, and purchases the finished product for 100, consisting of the 80 supply price and a 20 processing fee. In this simplified example, the original part and processing cost Toshiba 70. If the finished product remains unsold at period-end, using the markup of 30 to reduce current costs brings forward profit through the supply and repurchase arrangement. Analyze the supply, repurchase, and ultimate external sale together.

All four issues can overstate current profit, but each calls for different evidence. For long-term projects, check the cost estimates available at period-end. For promotional expenses, establish when the promotion took place. For semiconductor inventory, examine cost allocation and whether write-downs are required. For computer parts, review the supply and repurchase terms together.
