Chapter 1: What Is Corporate Governance? — Power, Responsibility, and Accountability

Core Theme: The essence of corporate governance is "accountability" — under the separation of power, ensuring that those who make decisions are held responsible for the consequences of those decisions.

Chapter Opening Case: Tesla Board and Elon Musk's Trillion-Dollar Pay Package

On November 6, 2025, Tesla shareholders approved an unprecedented compensation package: if Elon Musk can lead the company to achieve an 8.5 trillion USD market capitalization, deliver 20 million vehicles, 1 million robots, and 1 million robotaxis, and reach 400 billion USD in EBITDA over the next decade, he would receive stock options worth approximately 1 trillion USD (roughly 30 trillion TWD).

Yale professor Lucian Bebchuk, who served as an expert in the Delaware litigation over Musk's compensation, has long criticised the governance risks of such arrangements — enormous pay packages often reflect the board's susceptibility to management rather than pure performance incentives (Bebchuk & Kastiel, 2026). The performance thresholds are extraordinarily demanding (Tesla must become an $8.5 trillion enterprise), yet even if they are met, whether $1 trillion is "reasonable" remains an open question.

This case raises several fundamental questions:

  • Who actually has "control" of a company? The CEO, the board, or shareholders?
  • If the CEO is also a major shareholder (Musk holds approximately 13% of Tesla shares), how does this influence the design of compensation packages?
  • Why would a board approve a package that seems "impossible to achieve" but carries an astronomical price tag?

📌 Chapter Positioning Note: The Tesla pay case simultaneously involves "board independence" (Chapter 3) and "pay-performance decoupling" (Section 3.4) at technical levels. This chapter focuses on only one fundamental question: What are the boundaries of corporate governance? Compensation systems belong to the board's oversight technology, but they also involve value judgments about "who the corporation exists for" — that is the subject of Chapter 6 and beyond. This chapter first answers the first level — what the CG system "essentially" governs.

Section 1: What Is the Difference Between Corporate Governance and Corporate Management?

Imagine you opened a restaurant but are too busy to manage it yourself, so you hired a manager to run it. You hope the restaurant makes money, but the manager might care more about their salary, an easier workload, or even sneak home good ingredients for personal use. This divergence of interests is the "agency problem."

Corporate governance is a set of institutional arrangements designed to answer the following questions:

Question Corporate Management Corporate Governance
Who decides direction? Management Board of Directors (representing shareholders)
Who oversees execution? Board of Directors Shareholders, markets, regulators
How to prevent abuse of power? Internal control systems Board oversight + external audit + information disclosure
Time orientation? Short-to-medium-term operations Long-term strategy and risk
Success metric? Operational efficiency Whether accountability mechanisms work

In one sentence: Corporate governance determines "where the company should go" and "who oversees whether the direction is correct"; corporate management is responsible for "how to get there."

A company can be "well-managed" (efficient, low-cost) but "poorly governed" (lacking oversight, opaque information). Enron is a classic example — its energy trading business was managed exceptionally well, but its governance mechanisms completely failed.

Prentice Hall. on the distinction between "directed" and "managed"; and is further informed by Tricker, B. (2025). Corporate Governance: Principles, Policies, and Practices (5th ed.). Oxford University Press. on the essence of governance.

A. The OECD Definition

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD, 2023) provides the most widely cited definition of corporate governance:

"Corporate governance refers to the system by which companies are directed and controlled. Corporate governance specifies the distribution of rights and responsibilities among different participants — such as the board, managers, shareholders, creditors, auditors, and others — and spells out the rules and procedures for making decisions on corporate goals and performance monitoring."

This definition has several keywords:

Institutional Arrangement: Corporate governance is not a single law but an institutional system composed of laws, regulations, contracts, and conventions. Like a ball game that needs rules, referees, and player agreements to proceed smoothly.

Multiple Participants: Not just shareholders and management, but also boards, creditors, auditors, and more. Each party plays a different role, balancing one another.

Rights and Responsibilities: Clear delineation of what rights and responsibilities each party holds. Unclear authority and responsibility are the root of many corporate governance problems.

Goals and Oversight: Not only setting goals but also having oversight mechanisms to ensure goals are achieved. Goals without oversight are merely wishes.

B. Shleifer and Vishny's Definition

Shleifer and Vishny (1997) define corporate governance from the "capital provider" perspective:

"Corporate governance deals with the ways in which suppliers of finance to corporations assure themselves of getting a return on their investment."

This definition is more direct: the core question of corporate governance is — how is the money invested protected? Shareholders give money to the company; will management misuse it? If so, who will stop it?

C. Why Do We Need a New Definition?

Each of the above definitions has its contributions, but also its limitations:

OECD's "institutional arrangement" describes the "form" of CG (what it is), but does not touch on the "essence" (why it exists). Knowing what "institutions" are is not the same as understanding "what problem the institutions are meant to solve."

Shleifer & Vishny's "capital provider protection" identifies the "purpose" of CG (protecting capital providers), but is too narrow — it cannot explain why modern CG also requires responsibility toward employees, the environment, and society. If CG exists only to protect capital providers, then employee strikes, environmental pollution, and product safety would all fall outside its scope.

We propose "accountability" as the essence of CG because:

  1. Inclusivity: Accountability can encompass both the "responsibility to shareholders" and "responsibility to all stakeholders" perspectives. Whether in the narrow or broad sense of CG, accountability is the common core.
  2. Dynamism: Accountability is not merely "preventing bad things" (control); it also requires "being responsible for consequences" (reward and punishment). Control is static; accountability is dynamic.
  3. Operationalizability: Accountability can be decomposed into three mechanisms — prevention, enforcement, and correction — providing an analytical framework for subsequent chapters.

This positioning is supported by foundational corporate governance scholarship. Cadbury (1992), in the landmark report that launched the modern governance movement, argued that accountability is the essential principle that connects board responsibilities to shareholder and societal expectations. Tricker (1994) similarly identified accountability as the core function of the board, distinguishing governance from management precisely through the mechanism of answerability for corporate conduct. More recently, Keay and Loughrey (2015) have theorized accountability as the defining characteristic of good governance, arguing that without effective accountability mechanisms, governance structures remain merely formalistic. These convergent perspectives reinforce our framing: accountability is not one concern among many but the constitutive principle of corporate governance itself.

D. Our Position: Accountability Is the Essence of Governance

Synthesizing the above definitions, we propose a simpler definition:

The essence of corporate governance is "accountability" — under the separation of power, ensuring that those who make decisions are held responsible for the consequences of those decisions.

This definition has three keywords:

Separation of Power: In modern enterprises, ownership (shareholders) and management (management) are usually separated. Shareholders provide capital, but managers decide how to spend it. This separation is a source of efficiency (professional management) but also creates the "agency problem" (divergent interests).

Accountability: The core of governance is not "control" but "accountability." The goal of control is "don't make mistakes"; the goal of accountability is "be responsible for consequences." Accountability is more proactive than control — it not only prevents bad things from happening but also requires good things to be rewarded and bad things to be investigated.

Link Between Decision and Consequence: Governance requires that those who make decisions (or their representatives) are responsible for the consequences of those decisions. If a decision succeeds, the decision-maker should be rewarded; if it fails, the decision-maker should bear the consequences.

E. The Realization of Accountability: Three Mechanisms

Accountability is not an abstract concept. Its realization relies on three complementary mechanisms:

Mechanism Time Direction Function Examples
Prevention Before the fact Reduce probability of errors Board oversight, information transparency, internal controls
Enforcement After the fact Confirm attribution of responsibility Audit accountability, clawbacks, director removal
Correction After the fact Remedy institutional defects Standard revision, governance evaluation, regulatory updates

The "guidance" in the OECD definition corresponds to the "prevention" mechanism — reducing the risk of decision errors through goal setting and strategic direction. Therefore, "accountability" as the core theme of this book does not exclude "guidance" functions but views guidance as a preventive tool of accountability.

F. The Scope of Corporate Governance: From Narrow to Broad

Narrow View: Corporate governance primarily deals with agency problems between shareholders and management, with the core being to ensure managers serve shareholder interests. This view is especially prevalent in common law countries (such as the US and UK), emphasizing "Shareholder Primacy."

Broad View: Corporate governance covers the relationships among all stakeholders, including shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, communities, governments, etc. This view is more common in civil law countries (such as Germany and Japan), emphasizing the balance of "Stakeholders."

Why does this distinction matter? Because it directly affects corporate decision-making direction. Companies under the narrow view may focus more on short-term stock performance; companies under the broad view need to balance multiple interests and pursue long-term sustainable development.

Section 2: Why Do Shareholders Need a System to Oversee Managers?

A. The Agency Problem: An Inevitable Product of the Separation of Ownership and Management

The agency problem (Agency Problem) is the cornerstone of corporate governance theory. This concept was first systematically proposed by Jensen and Meckling (1976).

Core Scenario: Imagine you opened a restaurant but are too busy to manage it yourself, so you hired a manager to run it. You hope the restaurant makes money, but the manager might care more about their salary, an easier workload, or even sneak home good ingredients for personal use. This divergence of interests is the agency problem.

Key Concept Explanations:

  • Principal: The capital provider, i.e., shareholders. Entrusts others to manage the company on their behalf.
  • Agent: The entrusted manager, i.e., management. Accepts the mandate to manage the company.
  • Information Asymmetry: Agents possess more detailed information about company operations than principals; it is difficult for principals to fully understand.
  • Goal Inconsistency: Principals want to maximize company value; agents may pursue their own interests (higher pay, easier work, more power).

In modern enterprises, the agency problem is more complex, mainly divided into three types (see Chapter 2, Section 3 for details):

Type I Agency Problem: Shareholders vs. Management

This is the most classic agency problem. Shareholders (principals) want to maximize company value, but management (agents) may pursue their own interests.

For example:

  • Moral Hazard: Management may not work hard, or may take excessive risks
  • Adverse Selection: When selecting management, shareholders may not fully understand their true capabilities
  • Case: Before the 2008 financial crisis, many Wall Street bank executives issued high bonuses to encourage short-term profits while ignoring long-term risks, ultimately leading to systemic crisis.

Type II Agency Problem: Large Shareholders vs. Small Shareholders

Large shareholders may use their control to seek personal gain at the expense of small shareholders. This is especially common in family-controlled enterprises:

  • Tunneling: Large shareholders transfer company resources to themselves or related parties through related-party transactions, high-price purchases, and low-price sales
  • Case: Some Taiwanese family businesses transfer resources from listed companies to unlisted family holding companies through complex intra-group transactions.

Type III Conflict: Interest Conflict Between Shareholders and Other Stakeholders

Shareholders and other capital providers (such as creditors) have different risk preferences, which may lead to interest conflicts:

  • Shareholders prefer high-risk, high-return investments (because they enjoy residual claims)
  • Creditors only want the company to repay principal and interest on time (because they don't share excess profits)
  • Asset Substitution Problem: After obtaining debt capital, shareholders may invest in riskier projects than creditors expected
  • Key Distinction: The first two types are "principal-agent" relationships; the third type is a risk allocation conflict among different capital providers.

B. Agency Costs: The Price of Solving Problems

Agency problems generate agency costs (Agency Costs), mainly consisting of three parts:

Monitoring Costs: Costs incurred by shareholders to oversee management. For example: fees for hiring external accounting firms to audit financial statements, costs of board operations, costs of establishing internal control systems.

Bonding Costs: Costs incurred by management to gain shareholder trust. For example: management voluntarily accepting performance evaluations, proactively disclosing financial information, foregoing certain investment opportunities that may harm shareholder interests.

Residual Loss: Even with oversight and bonding, the loss caused by the fact that agency problems cannot be completely eliminated. This is because:

  • Oversight and bonding cannot be perfect
  • Information asymmetry always exists
  • Human self-interest cannot be completely eliminated

📊 Numerical Example: Assume a listed company's total agency costs are \$10 million, including \$3 million in monitoring costs (audit fees, board operations, etc.), \$2 million in bonding costs (performance evaluation systems, information disclosure, etc.), and \$5 million in residual loss (investment efficiency loss due to agency problems). This \$10 million is the price of "the separation of ownership and management."

C. Mechanisms to Solve Agency Problems

To solve agency problems, various governance mechanisms have developed in practice, which can be divided into internal and external categories:

Internal Mechanisms (institutional arrangements within the company):

  • Board Oversight: The board of directors elected by shareholders represents shareholders in overseeing management
  • Management Compensation Incentives: Such as stock options, aligning management interests with shareholders
  • Ownership Structure Design: Such as manager shareholding, making managers also owners
  • Internal Control Systems: Ensuring the reliability of financial reporting and compliance of operations

External Mechanisms (oversight forces from outside the company):

  • Market Competition Pressure: Product market competition, manager market competition, capital market competition
  • External Audit: Independent accounting firms verify the reliability of financial statements
  • Regulation and Supervision: Securities exchange acts, supervision by financial regulatory authorities
  • Takeover Market: Poorly managed companies may be acquired; management faces the pressure of being replaced
  • Active Participation of Institutional Investors: Large institutional investors influence corporate decisions through voting rights and dialogue

Why so many mechanisms? Because no single mechanism can perfectly solve agency problems. Various mechanisms are complementary — when internal mechanisms are weak, external mechanisms can provide reinforcement; when the external legal environment is imperfect, internal mechanisms can serve as substitutes.

Section 3: Does Corporate Governance Serve Only Shareholders, or Must It Respond to Everyone?

A. From Shareholder Primacy to Stakeholders: The Core Divide

Traditional corporate governance theory centers on "Shareholder Primacy," arguing that the sole goal of a corporation is to create maximum value for shareholders. The representative figure of this view is Nobel laureate in Economics Milton Friedman, who wrote in his famous 1970 article published in The New York Times: "The social responsibility of business is to increase its profits."

However, Freeman (1984) proposed "Stakeholder Theory" as a challenge to this view. He argued that companies should not serve only shareholders but should consider all individuals and groups affected by or able to affect corporate decisions.

B. Our Position: Expansion of Accountability Targets

In this book, we adopt the following position:

"Core" accountability targets of corporate governance are shareholders — because shareholders are the providers of capital and also the ultimate risk-bearers (in company liquidation, shareholders are last in the repayment order).

"Complete" accountability targets of corporate governance should expand to all stakeholders — because corporate decisions have far-reaching impacts on employees, customers, communities, and the environment.

CSR is a "qualitative expansion" of accountability targets — not just expanding from shareholders to more targets (quantitative change), but from "financial responsibility" to "social and environmental responsibility" (qualitative change). ESG is the modern language of CSR, used to "speak clearly" about these expanded responsibilities.

C. Multi-Level Accountability Framework

Accountability targets can be understood through a multi-level framework. The figure below shows the levels of accountability mechanisms, not the ranking of importance — accountability for the environment and future generations (such as carbon pricing, climate litigation) may be harder to achieve than shareholder accountability, but are equally important:

Level Targets Accountability Mechanisms
Level 1 Shareholders (ultimate risk-bearers) Board oversight, shareholder meetings, stock performance
Level 2 Direct contractual parties: employees, customers, suppliers, creditors Labor regulations, consumer protection, contract management
Level 3 Indirectly affected parties: communities, governments, the environment Environmental regulations, carbon pricing, ESG disclosure
Level 4 Long-term sustainability: future generations, ecosystems Climate policies, biodiversity conventions

D. Theoretical Bridge: Four Theoretical Perspectives on the Expansion of Accountability Targets

This section proposes that "accountability targets should expand from shareholders to all stakeholders." This position does not emerge from nowhere but is built upon the complementarity of four major governance theories:

Agency Theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) reminds us that the original problem of corporate governance is the conflict of interest between shareholders and management; but if we limit ourselves to the "shareholders vs. management" duet, we ignore that other stakeholders also face information asymmetry and the risk of interest violation.

Stewardship Theory (Davis, Schoorman, & Donaldson, 1997) provides another image: management is not necessarily self-interested; under appropriate institutional conditions, they can and are willing to serve broader goals. This provides a behavioral foundation for the "expansion of accountability targets" — if managers can become "stewards" of all stakeholders, then governance mechanisms should not be designed solely for shareholders.

Resource Dependence Theory (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978) further points out that corporate survival depends on multiple resource providers (employees, suppliers, governments, communities, etc.), and governance mechanisms must reflect these dependency relationships, or the enterprise will lose critical resources.

Institutional Theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Scott, 2014) explains from the external environment why accountability targets necessarily expand: regulatory, social normative, and professional expectations — institutional pressures — drive enterprises to respond to broader social expectations.

These four theories collectively support the core proposition of this book: the expansion of accountability targets is not a moral appeal but a theoretical necessity. Chapter 3 will explore in depth how boards respond to the challenges of these four theories in practice, and analyze how different governance mechanisms transform the abstract concept of "accountability" into operational institutional designs.

Section 4: How Does Taiwan's Governance Model Differ from Other Countries?

A. Global Major Corporate Governance Models

There are several major corporate governance models globally, each with its own historical, cultural, and institutional background. The comprehensive description of the three governance models in this section mainly draws from La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, & Shleifer (1999) on ownership structure comparisons, Shleifer & Vishny (1997) on governance model reviews, and OECD (2023) latest principles.

1. Anglo-American Model

  • Characteristics: Dispersed ownership, emphasis on shareholder rights, active M&A market
  • Representative Countries: United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia
  • Advantages: Effective market mechanisms, high information transparency, better investor protection
  • Disadvantages: Short-termism (excessive focus on quarterly reports), excessive management power
  • 2025 Latest Developments: US SEC continues to strengthen ESG disclosure requirements; institutional investors actively participate in corporate governance (PwC, 2025; Russell Reynolds, 2025)
  • Implications for Taiwanese Enterprises: Taiwanese enterprises seeking to attract foreign investment need to improve information transparency and shareholder rights protection

2. German-Japanese Model

  • Characteristics: Concentrated ownership, cross-shareholding between banks and enterprises, emphasis on employee participation
  • Representative Countries: Germany, Japan
  • Advantages: Long-term orientation, stable labor relations, strong bank oversight
  • Disadvantages: Lower information transparency, small shareholder rights easily overlooked
  • 2025 Latest Developments: Germany continues to promote the Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG) (German Federal Government, 2023); Japan promotes corporate governance code reform (Japan Exchange Group, 2023)
  • Implications for Taiwanese Enterprises: Taiwanese enterprises can refer to Germany's co-determination system to enhance employee participation

3. Family-Controlled Model

  • Characteristics: Family control, high overlap of ownership and management
  • Representative Countries/Regions: East Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin America, Southern Europe
  • Advantages: High decision-making efficiency, long-term commitment, family reputation constraints
  • Disadvantages: Severe agency problems between large and small shareholders, succession risks
  • 2025 Latest Developments: East Asian countries continue to promote corporate governance reforms, strengthening independent directors and audit committee functions (OECD, 2025)
  • Implications for Taiwanese Enterprises: Taiwanese family businesses need to pay special attention to agency problems between large and small shareholders and strengthen independent director functions

B. Taiwan's Governance Model: Family-Dominated Hybrid Characteristics

Taiwan's corporate governance is essentially a family-controlled model—family control, high overlap of ownership and management—similar to family-controlled economies in East Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin America, and Southern Europe. However, Taiwan's family-controlled model has two unique features:

  1. High foreign ownership ratio (43.1%, end-2024): Far higher than typical family-controlled economies, meaning Taiwanese family businesses face strong oversight from international institutional investors.
  2. Mandatory ESG disclosure: Starting from 2025, the Corporate Governance Evaluation was transformed into an ESG Evaluation, all listed companies are required to compile sustainability reports, and gradually align with IFRS sustainability disclosure standards (Financial Supervisory Commission, 2021). This makes Taiwan one of the few family-controlled economies globally that mandates sustainability reporting.

Taiwan's ownership structure characteristics (prevalence of family control, high foreign ownership) will be analyzed in depth in Chapter 2, Section 2.

Chapter Summary

Core Points:

  1. Corporate governance is a set of institutional arrangements that regulate the rights and responsibilities of all parties within a company, ensuring fair, transparent, and efficient operation.
  2. The essence of corporate governance is "accountability" — under the separation of power, ensuring that those who make decisions are held responsible for the consequences of those decisions. Accountability is realized through three mechanisms: prevention (oversight and transparency), enforcement (appointment/removal and discipline), and correction (institutional amendment).
  3. The agency problem is an inevitable product of the separation of ownership and management, generating monitoring costs, bonding costs, and residual losses.
  4. Global governance models include Anglo-American, German-Japanese, and family-controlled; Taiwan has family-dominated hybrid characteristics.

Key Terms:

Chapter Closing Case: Enron's Systemic Governance Failure — Lessons Two Decades Later

Core Question: Corporate governance is a set of institutional arrangements that ensure power is not abused. When institutions completely fail, individual moral flaws magnify into systemic disasters.

Case Summary

Enron was founded in 1985 and developed from a natural gas pipeline company into one of the world's largest energy traders. In 2000, revenues exceeded $100 billion, and it was selected by Fortune magazine as "America's Most Admired Company" six times. In December 2001, Enron filed for bankruptcy protection — then the largest bankruptcy case in US history.

Investigations found: (1) Management used "Special Purpose Entities" (SPEs) to hide debt and inflate profits; (2) Board oversight failed, allowing conflicted transactions; (3) External auditors (Arthur Andersen) provided both audit and consulting services, violating independence; (4) Analysts continued to recommend buying despite deteriorating financial conditions.
